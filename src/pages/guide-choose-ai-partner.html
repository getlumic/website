How to choose an AI implementation partner.

An AI implementation partner builds AI into the work a business already does, then keeps it running. A good one is easy to tell apart with a few plain questions. This checklist covers them, whichever partner you choose.

The checklist

Eight questions, before you sign.

  1. On site, or remote?

    Ask where the work will be mapped. A partner who sits beside the person doing the job sees the screens, the checks and the exceptions that never make it into a process document. Remote work can fit once a job is mapped, but a partner who has never watched the job done by hand is guessing at the hard parts. Remote fits better when the job is already written down step by step and the systems all run in the cloud.

  2. Built inside your systems, or a license to set up?

    Some partners build inside the ERP, accounting software, email and spreadsheets a team already runs. Others sell a software license and leave the setup to your staff. Ask which one you are buying, who does the configuration, and whether your team will have a new system to learn. A license can fit a team with staff to set it up and maintain it.

  3. Who approves before anything posts?

    An AI agent should prepare the work. A person who knows the work should approve it before it posts to the books or goes to a customer. Ask to see the approval step, what the agent may do on its own, and what it must never do without a person. Ask whether every action is logged, and who can read the log.

  4. What does monthly upkeep cover?

    AI needs upkeep. Vendors change their bill layouts, systems update their screens, and AI models improve. Ask what the monthly fee covers: monitoring, regular tests, fixes, upgrades and support. Ask how a problem is reported, who owns it, and when it counts as fixed.

  5. Can you compare the price before a long sales process?

    Starting prices, published or given on a short call, let you budget early. Ask for the rate of any paid discovery work and whether it is credited toward the build, how the build itself is priced, and the range of the monthly fee. A quote built from scratch fits better when the job is unusual and a starting price would mislead.

  6. How is the work kept running?

    Ask what happens on an ordinary night and on a bad morning. How are changes released, and is there a way back to the last good version? Does something watch each app and raise an alarm when it stops? Can an agent be paused, or its access revoked, at any time?

  7. Who owns what was built if you leave?

    Settle this before the build starts. Ask what stays with the business if the relationship ends: the written procedures, the configuration, the data and any dashboards. Ask how a hand-off would work, and who would hold the logins while it happens.

  8. What should the written proposal say?

    The proposal should name the one job to automate, the systems it will touch, who approves its work, the timeline, the one-time build cost, the monthly fee and what it covers, and what the business owns. If any of these is missing, ask for it in writing before you sign.
